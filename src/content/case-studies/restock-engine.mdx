## Summary

A kirana or small supermarket reorders several times a week, mostly from memory, and loses money in two directions at once: empty shelves on fast sellers and expired stock behind the counter. I built **ReStock**, a replenishment engine that takes four CSV exports from billing software and produces a daily order list where every line carries a reason the owner can check with a calculator. Before touching a real store, I built a simulator so every policy could be compared on identical demand. Status: benchmark complete, pilot pending. Source on [GitHub](https://github.com/lariyaharsh88/restock-engine).

## The problem

A shop with 300–1,500 SKUs reorders 3–7 times a week. Two failures happen together and the owner sees neither as a number: **stockouts on fast sellers** and **waste on perishables**.

Spreadsheet min–max rules make it worse in a hidden way. A product that keeps selling out looks like it has *low* demand, because an empty shelf records zero sales. The rule then orders even less.

<Callout type="insight">
An empty shelf is not zero demand. Any forecast that reads a stockout day as "nobody wanted this" will under-order the exact products that matter most. Censoring-aware demand estimation was the first design requirement, not a refinement.
</Callout>

## User

The owner-operator. They have cash pressure, no analyst, and no ERP. They have exports from billing software and a phone to call suppliers. **They will not trust a number they can't check.**

## Reframing the goal

<Callout type="decision">
I changed the goal from **"forecast demand better"** to **"produce an order the owner will send unchanged."** That one change put explanation on the critical path and ruled out any method that can't show its arithmetic.
</Callout>

Every line on the order list carries a short paragraph: recent daily demand, how many days of cover are on hand, the supplier lead time, the case pack, and why the quantity landed where it did.

## Options considered

<Options>
  <Option title="Machine-learned forecasting (gradient boosting, neural nets)">
    The segment has weeks of noisy data, not years. The models can't be explained to a shop owner, and they overfit the one store you train on. Rejected for a first product.
  </Option>
  <Option title="Auto-ordering: send purchase orders without approval">
    Removes the most work, but trust has to be earned first. One bad automatic order and the product is uninstalled. Rejected until a pilot proves line-acceptance rates.
  </Option>
  <Option title="POS and ERP integrations">
    Right eventually, but every integration is weeks of setup per billing vendor. Zero setup is what makes a first pilot possible. Rejected for now.
  </Option>
  <Option title="Explainable rules + a simulator to prove them" chosen>
    Censoring-aware smoothing, day-of-week factors, intermittent-demand handling, FEFO for perishables, and greedy margin-ranked cash allocation. Every step is auditable. Validated on a simulator before any pilot.
  </Option>
</Options>

## What I built

**1. Demand estimation that handles the messy parts.** Censored days (stockouts) are treated as "at least this much", not zero. Weekend peaks and intermittent sellers are handled separately.

**2. Order logic the owner can audit.** Reorder point and order-up-to level from demand, lead time and a service target, rounded to case packs and supplier minimums. Perishables use first-expiry-first-out so the engine never recommends stock that will expire before it sells.

**3. Cash allocation that is greedy on purpose.** When the weekly budget can't cover every line, lines are ranked by gross margin protected per rupee spent and filled in order. An optimal solver would do slightly better and be impossible to explain.

<Callout type="decision">
**Auditable beats optimal.** The owner has to sign off on the order. A greedy allocation they can follow line by line earns more trust than a solver output that is 2% better and opaque.
</Callout>

**4. A store simulator built first.** It includes the things that break spreadsheets: empty-shelf days, weekend peaks, promos, perishables with expiry, case packs, supplier minimums and unreliable lead times. Every policy sees identical demand, so the comparison is fair, and nothing is tuned on one shop.

## Trade-offs

<Callout type="tradeoff">
**Assisted vs. automatic.** Assisted ordering keeps a human on every order, which costs the owner a few minutes a day. I accepted that because the primary pilot metric is "lines sent unchanged", and only that metric can justify automation later.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tradeoff">
**Four CSV uploads vs. integrations.** Uploads are friction, but they work with every billing vendor on day one. Integrations come after the first pilot proves the order logic is worth integrating.
</Callout>

## Results

Simulated 120-SKU store, 5 seeds × 365 days. Three policies on identical demand.

| | Gut feel | Min–max spreadsheet | **ReStock** |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Fill rate | 90.7% | 98.1% | **98.1%** |
| Average inventory | ₹19.3L | ₹39.9L | **₹13.6L** |
| Perishable waste | 24.2% | 44.7% | **0.4%** |
| Gross margin, cash capped at 80% | ₹76.5L | ₹77.3L | **₹94.6L** |

## How I measured quality

- **19 hand-checkable unit tests.** Every worked example in the logic document is a test with the same numbers, so the documentation can't drift from the code.
- **Forecast eval on held-out seeds.** 7-day WAPE of 23.7% against 24.8% for a 28-day moving average.
- **No tuning on holdout.** The smoothing constant was chosen by a sweep on training seeds only.
