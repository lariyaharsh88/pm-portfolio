## TL;DR

Lead distribution was run by 40–50 people moving data through Google Sheets, one client at a time. Each additional client added more manual work. I reframed the problem from "make spreadsheets faster" to **"let the business grow clients without growing headcount"** and led the build of a Lead Management System (LMS). Ops teams set up a client's mapping once, choose real-time or scheduled delivery, and the system handles the rest, with masking, deduplication, exception tracking and audit logs built in.

<Metric value="23M+" label="duplicate sends blocked before reaching clients" />

## Context

KollegeApply generates student leads (website, Meta Ads, Google Ads, and external datasets of 5 lakh+ raw records a month) and delivers them to colleges and education partners. Every client has its own field mapping, course rules, daily caps and campaign limits, delivered via API or Google Sheets.

Admissions are seasonal. In April–May, volume and client demand peak at the same time. That is exactly when a manual process breaks.

## The problem

<Compare before="1–2 hours" after="≤5 minutes" label="Time to deliver one client's leads" />

Working with the operations team, I mapped the workflow end to end: **export → clean → filter → map → send → verify → report**, repeated for every client. One person could handle roughly five clients. That gave me seven problems, which grouped into four themes:

<WorkflowCompare />

1. **Scale.** Capacity grew linearly with people. With 400–500 regular clients, that meant 40–50 people.
2. **Risk.** Names, phone numbers and emails sat unmasked in shared sheets. Anyone with the link could see them.
3. **No traceability.** Nobody could answer "where did this lead come from, who received it, and why did delivery fail?" When an employee left, knowledge of their clients left with them.
4. **Slow everything else.** Reports were built from formula-heavy sheets that froze at scale. Communication (email, SMS, WhatsApp) caused duplicate sends. Even changing a website banner needed an engineering ticket and took 2–3 days.

<Callout type="insight">
The spreadsheet was a symptom. The real problem was that **operational knowledge lived in people's heads and hands**. Every rule a person applied manually (this client wants these courses, max 500 a day, no repeats) could be turned into configuration the system enforces.
</Callout>

## What we built

I owned problem discovery, requirements, prioritization, workflow design, QA sign-off and release planning. Engineering owned the architecture and implementation.

**1. Configure once, run continuously.** Ops sets a client's mapping and chooses real-time or scheduled delivery. After that, the system distributes leads on its own. The ops job changed from *doing* the delivery to *checking* it, about 10 minutes of monitoring a day.

<Figure src="/case-studies/lms-config.svg" alt="Client delivery setup: field mapping, real-time or scheduled delivery, and rules such as daily caps and PII masking" caption="Set up once per client: field mapping, delivery mode and rules (illustrative mockup, synthetic data)" />

<Callout type="decision">
**I chose configuration over automation scripts.** Scripts automate one person's workflow. Configuration lets anyone run any client's workflow. That is what made client continuity possible when people left.
</Callout>

**2. Masking by default.** PII is masked in the interface (`t************l`, `******7111`). Being able to open a record no longer means seeing the customer's details.

<Figure src="/case-studies/lms-console.svg" alt="Distribution console with masked names and phone numbers and a status for every record" caption="Masked PII and a status on every record (illustrative mockup, synthetic data)" />

**3. A full lead journey.** Every record carries its status history: received → validated → mapped → distribution attempt → outcome. User-level logs record who did what and when.

**4. Exceptions as first-class states.** Instead of "sent / not sent", the system distinguishes 10 states: success, duplicate, pending, failed, error, unknown, killed, daily limit exhausted, campaign limit exceeded, and course mapping error. Each state needs a different fix, and error alerts go out by email.

<Callout type="decision">
**I prioritized exception visibility before reporting dashboards.** At millions of records, an invisible failure is worse than a slow report. Knowing *why* something failed was the prerequisite for everything else.
</Callout>

**5. Communication tracking.** Previous sends and blocked contacts are recorded in the workflow, which reduced duplicate messages.

**6. Self-serve website inventory.** Banners, sponsored listings and featured listings moved into the CMS, so ops publishes them directly.

## Trade-offs

<Callout type="tradeoff">
**Masking vs. ops speed.** Some ops tasks occasionally need full contact details. We accepted that small amount of friction because exposing PII across 40+ users was the bigger risk. [TODO: describe how unmasked access is granted, e.g. role-based, and whether it is logged.]
</Callout>

<Callout type="tradeoff">
**Breadth vs. depth with 4 engineers.** I deliberately delayed polished analytics dashboards and advanced lead-quality scoring to ship distribution, masking and exception tracking first, because those removed the biggest operational bottleneck.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tradeoff">
**Strict deduplication vs. client volume.** Blocking duplicates means clients receive fewer records. We chose lead quality and client trust over inflated delivery counts.
</Callout>

## What I'd do differently

{/* TODO: these are suggested reflections. Keep only the ones that are true for you, and rewrite them in your own words. */}

- **Define success metrics on day one.** I had volume and time metrics, but no baseline for *lead quality* (contact rate and conversion at the client). That would have let me prove value to clients, not just to ops.
- **Investigate "unknown" and "pending" earlier.** About 3.7M records sat in these ambiguous states. Every unexplained state is a support ticket waiting to happen.
- **Build client self-serve reporting sooner.** Clients still asked our team for numbers they could have pulled themselves.

## If I built this today with AI

The rules engine would stay deterministic, because routing millions of leads needs predictable behavior. I would add AI where people still spend judgment:

1. **Mapping assistant.** An LLM proposes the field and course mapping for a new client from their sample file. A human approves it, and the system validates it against the schema. Target: onboarding drops from hours to minutes.
2. **Exception triage.** Classify and summarize error clusters ("72% of today's mapping errors come from one client's renamed course field") and suggest the fix.
3. **Lead-quality scoring.** Predict a lead's likelihood of conversion per client from historical outcomes.

**How I'd evaluate it:** mapping accuracy against a labeled set of past client configurations (target ≥95% field-level accuracy before auto-suggesting), false-positive rate for triage alerts, cost per onboarding, and a human-approval gate until accuracy is proven. Failure modes I'd watch: confidently wrong course mappings, and PII leaking into prompts. Masking happens *before* any model call.
